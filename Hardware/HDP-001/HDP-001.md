### 1.3 The budget, and which datasheet figure feeds which part of it

The 18″ has to cover everything: mechanics, camera-to-bore calibration, heat shimmer and the base.
The mechanics get **≤ 8″ per axis, settled and holding**. Two loops share that 8″, and that is why
a datasheet's separate figures matter by different amounts:

- **The joint's own loop** is the drive, closed on the output encoder at ≥ 1 kHz. It removes what
  the output encoder can see: gear wind-up, friction steps and lost motion. What is left depends on:
  - the encoder's resolution, repeatability and short-range error;
  - the stiffness, which sets how far a disturbance moves the output before the loop pulls it back;
  - the settle time.

  These figures carry the 8″: rows A1, A4, A6–A7, A10, B1 and E1–E4.
- **The narrow-camera loop** (§6.2) sees the target and the bore together, and corrects at 10–20 Hz
  (P6). It removes what no encoder can see: bearing tilt, base drift and calibration error. It
  also removes the joint's own errors in where it ends up, both its absolute error and its scatter
  (repeatability), because both stay fixed once the joint has arrived. So **neither absolute
  accuracy nor repeatability has to reach 8″**. They only have to:
  - put a cued target inside the narrow camera's view;
  - put a return to a known point on the target for a first shot taken before the camera has
    corrected anything (rows A2, A3).

So what decides the 8″ is what happens after the joint arrives: resolution, short-range error,
stiffness and settling. A gear catalogue's *transmission accuracy* (its input-to-output error) stops
mattering once the loop is closed on the output. It matters only for a part that closes the loop on
its motor encoder, and G2 rejects such a part.

### 1.5 Reading a datasheet against §1.4

- **"Accuracy" means three different things:**
  - a gear catalogue's *transmission accuracy* (传动精度) is the input-to-output error, which does
    not matter here (§1.3);
  - an encoder's *accuracy* is the encoder alone, often before it is installed (A5);
  - a joint's *positioning accuracy* (定位精度) is the joint with its encoder (A3).
- **Repeatability is usually measured from one side.** Gear catalogues approach the point from one
  direction. Reversing adds the lost motion, unless the loop is closed on the output (G2). Ask which
  the figure is (A2).
- **Resolution is not accuracy.** "19-bit" means 2.5″ per count. It says nothing about the ±45″
  error in §3's reseller listing.
- **Output or motor?** Listings, Chinese ones especially, often give the motor-side encoder bits and
  the motor rpm. Ask for the 输出端 (output-side) figures. The speed rows here are output rpm.
- **Rated, peak and momentary torque are different rows (D1–D3)**, as are rated and no-load speed
  (C1, C2).
- **Torsional stiffness (B1) is not the bearing's tilt stiffness (B2).** A joint's "rigidity" can be
  either, and both are quoted in N·m/rad or N·m/′, so ask which axis it is about.
- **Joints rarely print K1.** Ask for it, or take it from the gear size class and name the class
  (F1). §8 marks such values *class*.

## 2. The key decision: a gearbox on both axes, chosen for stiffness, not torque

The brief says "gearbox only if torque is insufficient". The station's own data says otherwise. The
GM6020 had plenty of torque (breakaway 0.6–0.7 A against a 3 A limit), yet yaw could not settle
better than 0.3°. The crossed-roller bearing's breakaway friction is 6–18× its sliding friction, so
every correction from rest lurches. To hold 8″ (39 µrad) against that 0.44 N·m step, a direct drive
needs a stiffness of 0.44 / 39×10⁻⁶ ≈ 11,000 N·m/rad from its control loop alone. With yaw's inertia
(0.033 kg·m²) that is a **~90 Hz position loop**: not realistic with this mechanics.

A 100:1 strain-wave gear supplies that stiffness mechanically (size 20: K1 ≈ 1.6×10⁴ N·m/rad at
80:1 and above, datasheet; 1.3×10⁴ is the 50:1 figure) and multiplies the motor's inertia by 10⁴ at
the output, so the same friction step deflects the output ~6″ and the output-encoder loop removes it
slowly. Its own crossed-roller output
bearing also replaces the current bearing.

**Decision: zero-backlash strain-wave gearing on both axes, with the position loop closed on an
output encoder.** Precision cycloidal (Spinea TwinSpin, lost motion < 1′) is technically equivalent
but no integrated hollow CAN joint was found at this size.

velocity and position loops on its own output encoder. controld becomes the **CANopen master**:
- NMT start, SDO configuration, PDO mapping at boot;
- CiA 402 state machine, cyclic synchronous position (CSP): a target per axis at 200 Hz on SYNC,
  position/velocity/current/status/temperature back;
- fault and warning words mapped onto the existing fault/hold/degrade ruling.
This is a new `MotorBackend` in controld, comparable in size to the current mixed CAN backend.
CiA 402 CSP over CANopen is confirmed by ZeroErr's *eRob CANopen and EtherCAT User Manual V1.9*.
The actual position (0x6064) is the output encoder, and the joint runs full closed loop on it. The
minimum SYNC period and the servo loop rates are not stated: ask ZeroErr.

**Consequences for the station software:**
- **No homing.** Both output encoders are absolute: a one-time zero calibration replaces sensorless
  homing, and the soft limits come from configuration, not from end stops. The joint has no homing
  mode (CiA 402 mode 6 is not supported), and none is needed.
- **No rest-stop park.** Park becomes a pose; Shutdown needs a holding brake on pitch if the payload
  is not balanced (owner ruling: never de-energise an unbalanced load). The eRob has a friction
  brake option (order code B), but its holding torque is not stated: ask. Balancing pitch is required
  either way.

**Risks to test first (§7, phase 1):**
- strain-wave hysteresis around reversal: a dual loop with an integrator can hunt in that dead zone;
- whether the delivered HS unit settles and holds to 8″ (§1.4 A1, A7), and meets A2's ±20″. ZeroErr
  quotes ±7″ from the lab and about ±15″ as typical, and guarantees neither (§8);
- 18 mm bore: it passes a cable bundle (48 V, CAN, Ethernet), not a slip ring. The slip ring sits
  below the yaw joint.

## 6. What the brief did not cover

1. **The gearbox criterion is stiffness, not torque** (§2).
2. **A narrow camera on the pitch stage, aligned with the rangefinder.** The wide camera resolves
   0.72 mrad per pixel = 2.5 MOA; a 46 cm target at 1500 m is under half a pixel. It cannot find the
   target, nor tell 0.3 MOA of aim, at that range. 0.3 MOA needs a sensor that sees the target and the
   bore together: a narrow camera (~15–30 µrad per pixel, a 200–300 mm lens) on the payload.
   The wide camera on yaw stays for search.
3. **A yaw-mounted camera changes the kinematics.** Today both cameras ride the pitch stage
   (`R_P_C`). A camera on the yaw stage does not tilt with pitch, so its relation to the bore changes
   with the pitch angle: a fixed offset cannot express it. Each camera needs to name the link it rides
   on (yaw or pitch) and its extrinsic on that link; the solver then aims the bore, carried by pitch,
   at a line of sight measured from the yaw frame. Software work, listed in §7.
4. **What crosses the slip ring.** Camera links (CSI, USB 3) do not survive a slip ring. Put the Pi
   and the Hailo on the yaw stage with the cameras; the ring then carries **48 V, CAN to the yaw joint
   (whose body sits on the base), and Ethernet** (or use Wi-Fi). Classic CAN at 1 Mbit/s with two
   joints at 200 Hz is ~20% bus load, with margin for a slip ring's contacts.
5. **Power.** The joints are 48 V: a supply sized for both peaks, separate from the 5 V for the Pi.
   This also ends the current 5 V droop.
6. **Brake and balance** for the heavy-payload shutdown (§3).

   **Proposed (2026-10-31), for the owner's decision: a small deliberate pitch imbalance.**
   - *The problem:* a balanced pitch keeps the gear's torque near zero, and wind then flips it from
     one tooth flank to the other. Any lost motion or backlash then shows in the aim, and a loop on
     the output encoder can hunt across it.
   - *The proposal:* offset the payload's centre of gravity by 5–8 mm. That gives 0.3–0.5 N·m at
     5–7 kg, more than the ≈ 0.14 N·m of an 8 m/s gust (P4), so the gear stays on one flank. It is
     still within P5's 10 mm and far below a 100:1 strain-wave gear's back-driving torque (29–59 N·m).
   - *Effect:* for a joint that closes its loop on the motor encoder (MZ17, case B in §8.7), it
     removes most of the backlash term. For any joint, it reduces reversal hunting (§3, risks).
   - *Cost:* a constant holding current (small), and pitch is then by definition out of balance at
     shutdown, so **the pitch brake (D5) becomes a must**.
7. **Calibration procedure for 0.3 MOA:** each camera's offset (now one per camera in
   `alignment.camera_offset`), the rangefinder-to-narrow-camera boresight (the 1535 nm beam is
   invisible: range a small target at distance while scanning, or use a camera that sees 1.5 µm),
   and the encoder zero.
8. **Heat shimmer at the venue** can be tens of µrad: measure it with the narrow camera before
   paying for more precision.

### 8.2 Trade-offs

1. **Integrated (eRob) or built (SHG-20 + encoder + drive).**
   - *eRob:*
     - one part, 1.5 kg, about USD 1.4–2k;
     - CiA 402 CSP confirmed, with the loop on the output encoder;
     - its precision is a vendor figure that the vendor itself says is not guaranteed (typical
       ±15″ / ±100″);
     - the encoder is fixed at 20 bit, the I type is IP54, the loop rates are unstated, and so is
       whether the 18 mm bore's wall stays still.
   - *Built path:*
     - the encoder can be chosen (RESA30: 26 bit, ±12.7″; REXA30: ±2″);
     - the drive's CiA 402 CSP and dual loop at 25 kHz are documented;
     - the housing sets the IP rating and the bore.
     - The cost is two axes of mechanical design. Encoder concentricity alone sets A5. The axis is
       heavier (≈ 2.5–3 kg) and has more parts, three of them unpriced.
   - The built path buys margin on A3–A6, which the camera loop makes less important (§1.3). It pays
     only if phase 1 finds the eRob's settled error, hold jitter or short-range error is the limit
     (A1, A6, A7).
2. **eRob90 or eRob80, per axis.**
   - Yaw needs the eRob90. The eRob80's 1.0×10⁴ is exactly on yaw's must, which leaves no margin for
     the friction step plus wind.
   - Pitch is the real choice. The eRob80 meets pitch's rec, and saves ~0.5 kg on the yaw stage
     plus about USD 150–800. One part type on both axes gives one spare, one configuration and one
     set of phase 1 tests.
3. **Encoder option.** Order **HS**: it is 20 bit (A4), it is compensated after assembly, and it is
   single-turn, so it needs no battery (G2). The standard 19-bit encoder misses A4. The multi-turn
   M/HM versions add a battery that nothing here needs.
4. **I type or T type eRob.** The T type's IP65* costs Ø96 × 124 mm and 2.6 kg, against
   Ø90 × 76 mm and 1.5 kg. An I type under a cover is the lighter way to meet G4.
5. **TwinSpin.** It is the stiffest per kilogram (2.4×10⁴ from a 1.05 kg gear), but:
   - it is not hollow (13 mm at best), so it suits pitch only;
   - its standard lost motion (1.5′) misses A9, and only the G option passes;
   - no integrated CAN joint exists, and pitch would become a different build from yaw.

   Not worth it unless pitch's stiffness turns out to be the limit.
6. **MyActuator RH.** It is light and CAN-driven, but it fails A4 and G1 (36″ steps), and its
   repeatability is 36″. It stays rejected (§5) whatever the price.
7. **MZ17 (the MZ17-mini listing), judged on the maker's preliminary sheet (§8.6).**
   - *What it is:* a strain-wave joint. The 51:1 and 101:1 ratios are the ones a strain-wave gear
     gives when the output is taken from the circular spline.
   - *For it:*
     - the lightest and smallest candidate (0.95 kg, Ø68 × 75.6 mm);
     - 15″ backlash stated;
     - 33 / 82 N·m at 101:1, ahead of the eRob80's 31 / 70;
     - the only joint that states its loop rates (current 50 kHz, velocity 10 kHz).
   - *Against it:*
     - **its 19-bit encoder misses A4's 20-bit must**, the same line the standard eRob misses;
     - **its Ø12 mm bore is below yaw's 15 mm (F5)**. Thin cables would fit through it, but its
       size 17 stiffness leaves no margin on yaw anyway (B1).
   - *Not stated:* repeatability, accuracy, K1, the CAN protocol and its position unit, a brake, the
     IP rating, and whether the output encoder closes the position loop. Humanoid joints are built
     for torque density and dynamic control, and they can run the loop on the motor encoder, using
     the output encoder only for absolute position at power-up. Here that would leave the 15″ of
     backlash outside the loop (G2), and that 15″ dead band alone is larger than the 8″ budget.
   - *Verdict:* a **pitch** candidate against the eRob80, on four conditions:
     - the maker offers an output encoder of ≥ 20 bit;
     - that encoder closes the loop;
     - the CAN protocol carries position finer than 1″;
     - repeatability is stated.

     Order 101:1, not 51:1:
     - it is stiffer: Harmonic Drive's size 17 is 1.0×10⁴ at 100:1, against 0.81×10⁴ at 50:1;
     - it has twice the motor-side resolution (A10) and twice the torque;
     - 30 rpm is plenty (C1).
   - *The MZ20 sibling* (Ø83 × 83.8 mm, 1.35 kg, 53 / 120 N·m at 30 rpm) is the yaw size. Its bore
     and encoder are not on the sheet.
   - *Accuracy:* §8.7. It is about 0.25 MOA in all if the loop closes on the output encoder. It is
     about 0.30 MOA (pitch only) to 0.40 MOA (both axes) if it does not.

### 8.3 Verdict

§3 stands, and the comparison sharpens the order:
- **Yaw:** eRob90, SHG gearing, 100:1, I type, **HS** encoder, **CANopen**, no brake, under a cover
  for IP.
- **Pitch:** the same part with a brake (B), or the eRob80 equivalent if the payload's mass
  (phase 0) makes the 0.5 kg matter.
- **Pitch, on price (owner, 2026-10-31):** the eRob90 costs almost twice the MZ17, and slightly lower
  accuracy is acceptable if worth it. On the estimate of §8.7, the MZ17 on pitch costs about
  0.01 MOA if its loop closes on the output encoder, and about 0.06 MOA if it does not. It joins
  the choice once its maker clears the four conditions in §8.2 item 7, and offers a brake. The
  options, with cost and estimated accuracy, are in [`servo-selection.md`](servo-selection.md) §4.
- Confirm the exact order code with ZeroErr.

### 8.7 What accuracy the MZ17 gives (estimate, 2026-10-31)

| Holding in wind, pitch / yaw | ≈ 3–4″ / 4–5″ | ≈ 10–12″ / 15–17″. Pitch ≈ 4–6″ with the preload of §6.6 |
| Against the 8″ per-axis share (§1.3) | meets it | misses it |

For the mount as a whole, assume calibration, heat shimmer and the base take ≈ 14″, which is what
the plan's split leaves them:

| | Total | At 1500 m | 0.3 MOA? | On the IPSC target (±32″)? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| eRob90 yaw + MZ17 pitch, case A | ≈ 15″ ≈ 0.25 MOA | ≈ 11 cm | yes | yes |
| eRob90 yaw + MZ17 pitch, case B | ≈ 18″ ≈ 0.30 MOA (0.26 with the preload) | ≈ 13 cm | on the line | yes |
| MZ17 on both axes, case B | ≈ 24″ ≈ 0.40 MOA | ≈ 17–19 cm | no | yes, with little margin for beam divergence |

A first shot taken before the camera has corrected anything is weak in either case. The sheet
gives no absolute accuracy. ZeroErr's standard 19-bit encoder, for comparison, is ±25″ in the lab
and about ±300″ in the field. Range only after the narrow camera has closed the loop.
